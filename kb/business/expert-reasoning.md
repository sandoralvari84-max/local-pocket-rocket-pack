# Business strategy — expert reasoning layer (how a business expert thinks)

Business reasoning is reasoning about scarce resources under uncertainty: where to spend the next euro and hour for the
most durable return. The skill is marginal thinking and honest unit economics, not grand vision statements.

## Decision method (think at the margin)
- Decide on the next increment, not the average: is the next euro of spend, the next hire, the next feature worth more than its best alternative? Averages hide the decision.
- Sunk costs are irrelevant to the forward decision. "We already spent €50k" is not a reason to spend €50k more; only future cost vs future benefit counts.
- Opportunity cost is the real cost of every choice: what you give up by not doing the next-best thing.
- Future money is worth less than today's (discount it); a stream of cash has a present value, and a big payoff far away may lose to a smaller one soon.

### Worked case — "should we keep funding this project?"
- Ignore what it has already cost (sunk). Ask only: from here, what will it cost to finish, and what will it then earn?
- Compare that forward return to the best alternative use of the same money and people (opportunity cost). If the alternative wins, stop — however painful.
- Check the unit economics, not the headline: does one more customer earn more than they cost to serve and acquire (LTV > CAC, with a sane payback period)? If not, growth makes the loss bigger.
- Decide with the present value of the forward cash flows, and state the assumption the case rests on (the one that, if wrong, flips the answer).

## Moats and competition
- A business is only durable if the advantage compounds or is hard to copy: network effects, switching costs, scale economics, a real brand, or proprietary data. "We work harder" is not a moat.
- Price on value to the customer, not on cost-plus; cost sets the floor, value sets the ceiling.
- Beware commodity markets with no moat: competition drives margin to zero regardless of effort.

## The meta-rule
An expert makes the assumptions explicit and quantifies the bet: the number that, if wrong, changes the decision, and how
confident they are in it. A strategy you cannot express as "we are betting X because we believe Y" is not a strategy.
