# Business — specialist layer (the finance and strategy facts models fumble)

Precise definitions and the reasoning errors that sink decisions.

## Decision-making traps
- **Sunk cost fallacy**: money already spent is irrelevant to the forward decision — only future costs/benefits count.
- **Opportunity cost**: the real cost of any choice is the best alternative forgone, not the cash outlay.
- Think **at the margin**: decide by the cost/benefit of one more unit, not averages.

## Corporate finance, stated correctly
- **NPV > 0 → invest** (at the right discount rate). NPV is the gold standard. **IRR is flawed**: multiple IRRs with
  unconventional cash flows, and it implicitly assumes reinvestment at the IRR — prefer NPV when they disagree.
- **Profit ≠ cash.** Accrual profit can be positive while the company runs out of cash (growth ties up working capital).
  Many profitable businesses die of cash flow. Watch runway and the cash-conversion cycle.
- **Contribution margin** = price − variable cost; it covers fixed costs then becomes profit. Break-even = fixed costs
  ÷ contribution margin. Gross margin ≠ net margin ≠ contribution margin — don't conflate them.

## Strategy, precisely
- Porter: five forces (rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, supplier power, buyer power) assess **industry
  attractiveness**; generic strategies are cost leadership, differentiation, focus — "stuck in the middle" loses.
- A **moat** (durable advantage) comes from network effects, switching costs, economies of scale, intangibles
  (brand/IP), or cost advantages — not from "working harder" or a first-mover claim (first movers often lose).
- Strategy = a diagnosis + a guiding policy + coherent actions (Rumelt). A list of goals is not a strategy.

## Evidence hygiene
- Business books suffer **survivorship bias** ("successful firms did X" ignores the failures that also did X) and
  **correlation-as-causation**. Treat "secrets of success" lists skeptically.
- Price by **value / willingness to pay**, not cost-plus; elasticity decides whether a price cut raises or lowers profit.
