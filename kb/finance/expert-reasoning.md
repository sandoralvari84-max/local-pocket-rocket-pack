# Finance — expert reasoning layer (how a finance expert reasons)

Educational reasoning method, not personalised financial advice. The through-line is risk-adjusted thinking: no return
figure means anything without the risk taken to get it, and the future is a distribution, not a point.

## Core method
- Separate return from risk-adjusted return. "20% last year" is noise until you know the volatility, the drawdown, and whether it survives a bad year. Compare on risk-adjusted terms.
- Discount the future: a euro in ten years is worth less than a euro now; value any plan by the present value of its cash flows, and state the discount rate as an assumption.
- Diversification is the one near-free lunch: uncorrelated holdings cut risk without cutting expected return. Concentration is a bet you must consciously choose, not drift into.
- Costs and taxes compound against you exactly as returns compound for you; a 1%/yr fee is enormous over decades.

### Worked case — reasoning about "is this investment good?" (educational)
- Reframe to risk-adjusted and after-cost: what return, for what volatility and worst-case drawdown, net of fees and tax? A high headline return with ruinous downside is not "good".
- Check correlation with what the person already holds: a great asset that moves with everything else adds less than a mediocre one that moves differently.
- Separate the base rate from the story: most active bets underperform a low-cost index after costs; the burden of proof is on the exception.
- Size by downside survivability: never a position whose worst plausible case you cannot absorb. Position sizing beats being right.

## Behaviour and base rates
- The big destroyers of returns are behavioural: buying high on euphoria, selling low on fear, and overtrading. A boring rule beats a clever impulse.
- Past performance is weak evidence of future performance; reversion to the mean is the stronger force.
- If it promises high return with low risk, the risk is hidden, not absent. Find it.

## The meta-rule
An expert states the assumptions (return, risk, horizon, costs) and the scenario that breaks the plan, and never confuses a
single outcome with a good decision. This is general education; a licensed adviser who knows the individual's full situation should sign off on any actual decision.
