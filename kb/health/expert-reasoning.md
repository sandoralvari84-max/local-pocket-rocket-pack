# Health — expert reasoning layer (how a clinical thinker reasons)

Educational reasoning method, not medical advice, not a diagnosis, and not a substitute for a clinician who can examine the
person. The through-line is evidence hierarchy, base rates, and knowing the red flags that mean "stop reasoning, seek care now".

## Core method
- Separate symptom from diagnosis: many conditions share a symptom. Reason about the differential (the set of plausible causes) and their base rates, not the first scary possibility.
- Weigh evidence by quality: a systematic review or large randomised trial outranks one study, which outranks mechanism/anecdote. "A study showed" is weak until you know which kind and how big.
- Think in base rates and absolute risk: "doubles the risk" is meaningless without the starting risk. A rare event doubled is still rare; a common one slightly raised can matter more.
- Benefit and harm together: every intervention has both. The question is net benefit for this situation, not benefit alone.

### Worked case — reasoning about "is this supplement/remedy worth it?" (educational)
- Ask what the good evidence says, not what the marketing or a testimonial says: look for randomised trials and reviews, and note the effect size, not just "helps".
- Check the base rate and whether the claimed effect is clinically meaningful (a statistically significant tiny effect can be useless in practice).
- Weigh plausible harms and interactions, and the opportunity cost of using it instead of something proven.
- Conclude with calibration: "limited evidence, small or unclear benefit — discuss with a clinician, especially alongside other medications," not a verdict.

## Red flags (reasoning stops, care begins)
- Some presentations are not for reasoning-it-out: chest pain, trouble breathing, sudden severe headache, signs of stroke (face/arm/speech), severe or worsening symptoms, or anything acute in an infant, elderly, pregnant, or immunocompromised person → urgent professional care.
- Recognise the limit of remote reasoning: no examination, no tests, no full history. State that limit plainly.

## The meta-rule
An expert reasons from the quality of evidence and the absolute risk, states the uncertainty, and knows when to stop and
refer. This content is general health information for education only — not a diagnosis or treatment plan; a qualified clinician who can assess the person should guide any decision.
