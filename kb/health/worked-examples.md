# Health — worked examples (mentor-curated, round 3: reasoning by example)

Method: restate → retrieve facts → reason in steps → check → concise answer. General, evidence-based education — NOT medical advice; it does not diagnose. See a clinician for personal concerns.

## Example 1 — "An ad says a supplement makes you '50% less likely to get sick'. How should I judge that?"
- Restate: evaluate a health claim critically.
- Facts (advanced.md › Evidence-based medicine): study-design hierarchy; RCT vs observational; absolute vs relative risk; sample size; publication bias.
- Steps:
  1. "50% less likely" is usually a *relative* risk reduction. If the baseline risk is 2%, a 50% cut is only 1 percentage point absolute — small. Ask for the absolute numbers.
  2. What's the evidence level? A large randomised controlled trial or systematic review is strong; a small or observational study or a testimonial is weak.
  3. Check sample size, who funded it, and whether it's peer-reviewed; association ≠ causation.
- Check: separates relative from absolute risk and weighs study quality; matches the KB; stays non-advisory.
- Answer (general): Treat "50% less likely" with caution — it's probably a relative figure that may be tiny in absolute terms. Look for a large RCT or systematic review and the absolute risk change before believing it. This isn't medical advice.

## Example 2 — "A test is 99% accurate for a rare disease and I tested positive. Am I probably sick?"
- Restate: interpret a positive result on a rare-disease screen.
- Facts (advanced.md › Epidemiology): sensitivity/specificity vs positive predictive value; base rates (prevalence) drive PPV.
- Steps:
  1. "99% accurate" is not the chance you're sick. With a rare disease, most positives can be false positives.
  2. Example: prevalence 0.1% (1 in 1,000), 99% specificity → in 1,000 people ~10 false positives vs ~1 true positive. So a positive is right only about 1 in 11 (~9%).
  3. The rarer the condition, the lower the positive predictive value; a confirmatory test is usually needed.
- Check: applies base-rate reasoning (Bayes) correctly; arithmetic consistent; non-diagnostic.
- Answer (general): Not necessarily — for a rare disease, even a 99% test gives mostly false positives, so a single positive might mean only ~9% chance of disease. A confirmatory test is needed. Discuss your result with a clinician.
