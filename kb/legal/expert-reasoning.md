# Legal — expert reasoning layer (how a legal thinker reasons)

Educational reasoning method, not legal advice and not a substitute for a qualified lawyer in the relevant jurisdiction.
The through-line is IRAC: issue, rule, application, conclusion — and the discipline of naming the jurisdiction and the facts a conclusion depends on.

## Core method (IRAC)
- Issue: state the precise legal question, not the vague worry. "Can they do X?" becomes "does act X by party A breach duty Y owed to B under law Z?"
- Rule: identify the governing rule AND its source and jurisdiction. Law differs by country/state; a confident answer for the wrong jurisdiction is worse than "it depends on where".
- Application: apply the rule to the specific facts, both ways — the argument for and the argument against. The facts that change the outcome are the heart of the analysis.
- Conclusion: a calibrated answer ("likely", "arguable", "clearly") with the facts and assumptions it rests on, not a bare yes/no.

### Worked case — reasoning about "can I use this content / is this allowed?" (educational)
- Pin the jurisdiction and the exact conduct first; "is it legal" has no answer until you know where and what precisely.
- Find the governing rule and its exceptions: e.g. copyright protects expression with limited exceptions (and those exceptions, like fair use/fair dealing, are fact-specific multi-factor tests, not a word-count rule).
- Apply to the actual facts both ways: what cuts for permission (licence, exception, the idea not the expression) and what cuts against (substantial copying, commercial harm). Name the fact that would flip it.
- Conclude with calibration and a referral: "likely needs a licence unless an exception applies on these facts — confirm with a lawyer in your jurisdiction," not a false certainty.

## Risk and process reasoning
- Separate "is it lawful" from "is it worth the risk": cost, likelihood of enforcement, and reputational exposure are part of a real decision.
- Read the actual instrument (contract clause, statute, notice) before opining; the specific wording usually decides it.
- Deadlines and formalities are often decisive and unforgiving (limitation periods, notice requirements). Flag them early.

## The meta-rule
An expert states the jurisdiction, the governing rule, the facts the answer turns on, and the uncertainty — and refers out
to a qualified professional for anything consequential. This content is general legal information, not advice, and no lawyer-client relationship is created.
