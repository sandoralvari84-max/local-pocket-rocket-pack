# Law — worked examples (mentor-curated, round 3: reasoning by example)

Method: restate → retrieve facts → reason in steps → check → concise answer. General legal education, NOT legal advice; laws vary by jurisdiction — consult a qualified lawyer for a real matter.

## Example 1 — "A freelancer built our logo. Who owns the copyright?"
- Restate: determine default copyright ownership of commissioned work, generally.
- Facts (foundations › IP; advanced.md › Intellectual property): copyright arises automatically in the creator; transfers need a written assignment; "work for hire" rules vary by jurisdiction.
- Steps:
  1. By default in many jurisdictions the *creator* (the freelancer) owns the copyright, even though you paid for it.
  2. Ownership passes to you only with a written assignment (or a qualifying work-for-hire arrangement, which differs by country).
  3. So check the contract: is there an IP assignment clause? If not, you may hold only an implied licence, not ownership.
- Check: distinguishes paying-for vs owning; flags the written-assignment requirement and jurisdiction variance; not advice.
- Answer (general): Usually the freelancer owns the copyright unless your contract assigns it to you in writing. Add an IP-assignment clause; for your jurisdiction and situation, confirm with a lawyer.

## Example 2 — "We want to email EU users marketing. What does GDPR require?"
- Restate: outline GDPR obligations for marketing to EU users, generally.
- Facts (advanced.md › Data protection): lawful basis (consent or legitimate interest), transparency, data minimisation, data-subject rights, records.
- Steps:
  1. Establish a lawful basis — for marketing email, typically consent, or legitimate interest where it properly applies (and local e-privacy rules may require opt-in).
  2. Be transparent (clear privacy notice), collect only what you need, and make opting out easy in every message.
  3. Honour access/erasure/objection requests; keep records of consent.
- Check: hits lawful basis + transparency + minimisation + rights + opt-out; flags jurisdiction/e-privacy nuance; not advice.
- Answer (general): You need a lawful basis (usually consent for marketing email), a clear privacy notice, data minimisation, easy opt-out, and respect for data-subject rights. Local e-privacy rules matter too — verify specifics with a professional.
