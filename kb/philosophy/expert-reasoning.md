# Philosophy — expert reasoning layer (how a philosophical thinker reasons)

Philosophical skill is not knowing positions; it is handling arguments: making the strongest version of each side, finding
the hidden premise, and distinguishing what is true from what merely sounds persuasive. Clarity about the question comes before any answer.

## Core method
- Clarify the question and the terms first. Most disputes dissolve or sharpen once "free", "know", "good", "same" are defined; equivocation on a word is the commonest error.
- Reconstruct the argument as premises and conclusion. Then test it twice: is it valid (does the conclusion follow if the premises hold?) and are the premises true? A valid argument from a false premise proves nothing.
- Steelman before you criticise: state the opposing view in its strongest form, one its holder would accept. Beating a weak version teaches you nothing.
- Use thought experiments to isolate the variable (the intuition pump): change one thing and see if the judgement moves, to find what is actually doing the work.

### Worked case — reasoning about a hard question (e.g. "do we have free will?")
- First disambiguate: "free will" compatible with determinism (able to act on your own reasons without coercion) is a different claim from libertarian free will (undetermined choice). Many disputes are two people answering different questions.
- Lay out the main positions as arguments with premises (determinism, compatibilism, libertarianism), and find each one's load-bearing premise and its cost.
- Apply a test case: does moral responsibility require the ability to have done otherwise, or only that the act flowed from the agent? Your answer here largely settles the rest — so examine it directly.
- Conclude with calibration: which position is most defensible and why, what it costs you to hold it, and what would change your mind — not a slogan.

## Reasoning hygiene
- Name the fallacy when it appears (equivocation, begging the question, false dilemma, appeal to consequences) — but naming a fallacy is not refuting the conclusion; the claim may still be true for other reasons.
- Separate descriptive from normative: "is" does not by itself yield "ought". Watch the slide.
- Hold the question open when the arguments are genuinely balanced; forcing a verdict is a failure of the discipline, not a success.

## The meta-rule
An expert states the strongest case against their own view and what would change their mind. The mark of philosophical skill
is not certainty but the quality of the argument and the honesty about where it runs out.
