# Philosophy — introduction (mentor-curated seed, round 1)

## Main branches
- Metaphysics: what exists and the nature of reality.
- Epistemology: knowledge — what we can know and how we justify beliefs.
- Ethics: how we ought to act and what is good.
- Logic: valid reasoning and argument.
- Aesthetics: beauty and art. Political philosophy: justice, rights, the state.

## Critical thinking & logic
- An argument has premises and a conclusion. Valid = conclusion follows if premises are true; sound = valid + true premises.
- Deductive reasoning guarantees the conclusion; inductive reasoning makes it probable.
- Common fallacies: ad hominem, straw man, false dilemma, appeal to authority, slippery slope, circular reasoning, correlation≠causation.
- Charitable interpretation (steelmanning) strengthens debate; distinguish claims from evidence.

## Major ethical theories
- Consequentialism / utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill): maximise overall good/well-being; judge acts by outcomes.
- Deontology (Kant): some duties and rights hold regardless of outcome; act only on rules you could universalise.
- Virtue ethics (Aristotle): cultivate good character traits; the good life (eudaimonia) through virtue.
- Contractarianism (Hobbes, Rawls): morality/justice as principles rational people would agree to.

## Schools & figures (very brief)
- Ancient: Socrates (questioning), Plato (forms), Aristotle (logic, virtue), Stoicism (control what you can).
- Modern: Descartes (doubt, "I think therefore I am"), empiricism (Locke, Hume) vs rationalism (Leibniz), Kant.
- Later: existentialism (Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Sartre — meaning and freedom), pragmatism, analytic philosophy.

## Why it's useful
- Sharpens reasoning, clarifies values, exposes assumptions, and improves how you frame and resolve hard questions.

# Philosophy — advanced / academic layer (mentor-curated, round 2)

Builds on philosophy-intro.md.

## Logic (formal)
- Propositional logic: connectives, truth tables, validity; predicate logic: quantifiers, variables.
- Validity vs soundness; necessary vs sufficient conditions; modal logic (necessity/possibility).
- Formal fallacies (affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent) vs informal fallacies.

## Epistemology
- Classical analysis: knowledge as justified true belief; the Gettier problem challenges it.
- Justification theories: foundationalism vs coherentism; internalism vs externalism (reliabilism).
- Skepticism, the problem of induction (Hume), a priori vs a posteriori, analytic vs synthetic.

## Metaphysics
- Free will: hard determinism, libertarianism, compatibilism.
- Mind: dualism, physicalism/identity theory, functionalism; the hard problem of consciousness; personal identity over time.
- Universals vs particulars (realism/nominalism); causation; time.

## Ethics (three levels)
- Metaethics: moral realism vs anti-realism, cognitivism vs non-cognitivism, the is–ought gap (Hume), the open-question argument.
- Normative: consequentialism/utilitarianism (act vs rule), deontology (Kant's categorical imperative), virtue ethics (Aristotle), contractualism (Rawls, Scanlon).
- Applied: bioethics, trolley problems, justice and distribution, rights, animal ethics.

## Philosophy of science
- Demarcation; Popper's falsifiability; Kuhn's paradigms and normal/revolutionary science; Lakatos's research programmes; underdetermination; realism vs instrumentalism.

## Political philosophy
- Social contract (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau); Rawls's justice as fairness (veil of ignorance, difference principle) vs Nozick's entitlement theory; liberty (Mill's harm principle); theories of distributive justice.
